The 2026–27 Federal Budget tax reforms

Three tax changes.

1. Negative gearing

What changes

From 1 July 2027, rental losses on established investment properties bought after Budget night (7:30pm, 12 May 2026) can no longer be deducted against your salary. They're quarantined: carried forward and offset against rental income or capital gains from rental property instead. New builds keep full negative gearing — the reform redirects the concession toward adding housing supply.

Who it touches

In 2022–23, about 2.26 million individuals had an interest in a rental property — out of roughly 16 million who lodged tax returns. More than 8 in 10 taxfilers own no investment property at all, so for most people there is simply nothing here to lose. Negative gearing cost the budget about $10.9 billion in forgone revenue in 2023–24.

What's grandfathered

Every investment property acquired — by contract date — before Budget night is completely unaffected, forever. If you owned a rental on 12 May 2026, nothing about how you negatively gear it changes.

The common misunderstanding

Much of the alarm assumes existing investors lose their deductions. They don't. The change only applies to established properties bought after Budget night — a group that, by definition, didn't exist when the policy was announced and can plan around it.

2. Capital gains tax

What changes

From 1 July 2027, the 50% CGT discount is replaced with inflation indexation of the cost base — you pay tax only on real gains, the way Australia did from 1985 to 1999 — plus a minimum 30% rate on net capital gains.

Who it touches

Around 83% of the benefit of the current discount flows to the top 10% of taxpayers by income. If you're not regularly realising large capital gains, the change is close to invisible to you. Your family home remains exempt from CGT entirely — that hasn't changed.

What's grandfathered

Gains accrued before 1 July 2027 keep the 50% discount even if you sell years later. Only growth after that date falls under the new rules; a valuation at the changeover (or an ATO formula) splits the two.

The common misunderstanding

"Indexation means paying more tax" — usually it doesn't, for steadily appreciating assets. The new system taxes the real gain instead of half the nominal gain; which is larger depends on how fast the asset grew relative to inflation. The case where indexation genuinely bites — very fast growth from a near-zero cost base — is real, and covered honestly below.

3. Family trusts — and the "death tax" claim

What changes

From 1 July 2028, distributions from discretionary trusts are taxed at a minimum of 30%. This ends the main tax benefit of "income splitting" — routing a high earner's income through a trust to family members on low marginal rates.

Who it touches

About 810,000 adults — roughly 5% of individual taxfilers — received discretionary trust distributions in 2022–23. The Government's factsheet estimates more than 95% of taxfilers won't be affected in any given year. Of small businesses, fewer than 15% operate through a discretionary trust. And beneficiaries already paying 30% or more on their distributions see little change — the measure mainly affects distributions taxed below 30%.

Is it a death tax? No.

The 30% minimum applies to income distributions from discretionary trusts — not to inheritance, not to assets passing under a will, and not to deceased estates. Explicitly excluded: testamentary trusts in existence on 12 May 2026, deceased estates, special disability trusts, charitable trusts, fixed and widely held trusts, and complying super funds. Australia abolished inheritance taxes decades ago, and this Budget does not reintroduce one. Calling this a death tax is not an interpretation — it's a factual error.

The honest caveats

A fair accounting means acknowledging where the criticism has a point.

The startup problem is real

Indexation works well for assets bought at a meaningful price. It works badly for founder equity and early startup shares with a near-zero cost base: indexing almost-nothing for inflation gives almost-nothing, so nearly the entire gain is taxed at a minimum 30% — a genuinely worse outcome than the 50% discount. This deserves a targeted fix, not wholesale rejection of the reform, and the Treasurer has confirmed the Government is examining options for small and startup businesses, including retaining discount-like treatment for qualifying companies.

Other genuine pressure points

  • Valuation friction. Splitting pre- and post-July-2027 gains requires a valuation or an ATO formula. For unlisted assets that's real cost and uncertainty.
  • Small businesses in trusts. A minority of small businesses do run through discretionary trusts; for those whose beneficiaries are on low rates, tax goes up. The carve-outs (primary production income, vulnerable minors) catch some but not all hard cases.
  • The minimum 30% rate on gains is a higher floor than some middle-income, infrequent sellers pay today under the discount.
  • Renters and overall supply. The same Treasury modelling that forecasts 75,000 homes shifting to first home buyers also estimates overall supply about 35,000 dwellings lower over the decade and rents around $2 a week higher at the current median — modest, but not zero.

None of this changes the distributional core: each reform leaves the large majority of Australians untouched, and the benefits being trimmed flowed overwhelmingly to the highest earners. But edge cases are how good reforms get discredited — they're better acknowledged and fixed than denied.
